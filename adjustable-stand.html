It was made for every loop in the lab, prints in a couple of hours, and is shared on the lab's public drive. Project Overview

The lab needed a way to suspend piping from 0.25 to 1 inch in diameter at various heights, integrated with the 1 inch spaced holes in the benchtop. I designed and 3D printed a series of stand iterations, each one aimed at improving overall print quality, how easily the stand could be adjusted with one hand, and how well it would hold up over time.

The stand was made for all of the loops in the lab, including the cold plate loop. I designed it to be 3D printable and cut the print time down to a couple of hours from export to taking it off the print plate, so a new stand is a same-day or overnight job when one is needed. The design is on a public drive for all of the PhD students and postdocs working in the lab.

Why 10 Iterations

Each iteration came from something that failed or fell short when the part was actually printed and used, which is why the design changed mechanism partway through.

  1. Started with a rack and pinThe first version adjusted height with a rack and pin system.
  2. Tested push-down slidersI printed a couple of push-downs at various lengths to test fit with ridges, hoping the slider would act like a spring as it was pushed down. It didn't work well in real life. They were also a pain to get back out once pushed in, so reusing them would have been a hassle.
  3. Switched to a pinch sliderSqueezing to adjust is still doable with one hand, and it let me make the ridges deeper, which made the overall platform stronger. The pinch slider is also the easiest to make move in both directions, and it let the stand hold a greater weight before failing.
  4. Checked the structureThe side walls needed a slit that went all the way through, and I was concerned that would hurt structural integrity. It turned out to be fine, and adding fillets where the columns meet the plate at the bottom of the stand reduced potential bending even further.

Height-Adjustment Mechanisms

I tried three different ways of adjusting height: a pinch slider, a rack and pin system, and a one-way push slider. The pinch slider shown above became the final design; these are the alternatives that were built and tested along the way.

Slider Iteration

Every slider, first to last. Laid out in the order they were printed.

The slider is the part a user actually touches, so it went through the most revisions. Each version was printed, fit into the frame, and tested for how easily it adjusted and how securely it held before the next change was made.

  • Slider widthTuned so the slider moves smoothly in the frame without wobbling.
  • Pinch area sizingSized so the slider can be squeezed and moved with one hand.
  • Snap-fit ledges & groovesAdjusted so the stand locks firmly in place and won't fail, while still being easy to release.

Print Quality

The final pinch slider geometry was printed in two orientations. Orientation changed the quality of the finished part, so the better orientation was used for the final stand.

Bad orientation. The same slider geometry with noticeably worse print quality.

Snap-Fit Details

The frame uses slits with ledges and grooves that the slider snaps into. Getting these dimensions right was the balance between a stand that holds its height reliably and one that is still easy to adjust.

Snap-fit slits, close up. The ledges and grooves that hold the slider in position.

Material & CAD

The stand is printed in PLA, which had the right balance of springiness and bend-to-stiffness ratio to prevent warping over time. I modeled it in SolidWorks and built it up as a CAD assembly.
